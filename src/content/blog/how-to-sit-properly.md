You've probably heard that sitting is the new smoking, a claim that sounds like slogan invented to sell standing desks, but which, annoyingly, isn't entirely wrong. Most of us now spend more of our waking hours in a chair than anywhere else, an arrangement which our bodies were not built for.

It's true, your chair is probably killing you. Though, to be fair, so is everything else, just at varying speeds.

The usual response is to go hunting for the perfect chair and the perfect posture, and to feel frustrated about never quite achieving either. But after ten years of recommending ergonomic chairs to people, I've come to to realize the whole thing rests on a misunderstanding. The problem isn't really how we sit. It's how long we sit that way.

## There is no good chair

Here is a heresy for an industry that sells them: there is no such thing as a good office chair. There are only chairs that make it easier, or harder, to stop sitting the way you are sitting now.

The best posture is not a single position. It is the next one. Lean forward to write, lean back to think, perch, slump, cross your legs, uncross them.

The body is not designed to hold a pose. It is designed to move between them. A chair that locks you into an ideal is a beautifully engineered mistake.

## The tyranny of the right angle

Somewhere along the way, the ninety-degree angle became doctrine. Thighs level, back upright, knees square, as if the body were flat-pack furniture. It has the appeal of geometry and little else to recommend it.

Sit at a strict right angle and the pelvis rolls backwards. The lower spine flattens, and the gentle S-curve that holds you up collapses into a C.

A good chair does the opposite. It lets the pelvis tilt into its natural position, and the spine, spared its geometry lesson, keeps its natural curve on its own.

## Sitting is supposed to be restful

The second myth is that sitting well takes effort. It is a strange idea once said aloud. Sitting is meant to be relaxing. That is rather the point of a chair.

You do not need to brace your muscles all day to ward off ruin. A good backrest supports the spine's natural curvature even when you recline, which is when most of us do our best thinking. Leaning back is not a moral failing.

Which brings us to "active sitting", a phrase that should give pause. Active sitting means engaging your muscles. That is to say, working. As one posture among many, it earns its place. As a philosophy, it mistakes a chair for a gym.

## The armrest paradox

Almost every office chair has armrests. Almost nobody uses them. It is one of the minor mysteries of working life, and the answer is mundane: they are usually too long. They collide with the desk, so people lower them out of the way or take them off altogether.

Most modern chairs now let you slide the armrests back as far as they go, so that only the back part of the forearm rests on them. Then set the height so the weight of your arm settles on them, slightly. The trapezius, which has been quietly holding your arms up since breakfast, can finally let go.

Armrests do one more thing. Left to ourselves, we lean to one side and stay there. Two armrests at the same height keep us honest.

## In defence of the slouch

The body always drifts towards relaxation when it works. Give it an hour at a desk and it will find the path of least resistance. We call this slouching, and we say it with disapproval.

But the slouch is not a vice. It is just muscles clocking off. The trouble is not the posture but its duration. Hold any position long enough, even a virtuous one, and the strain collects somewhere else: the neck, the shoulders, the lower back.

So how do you sit properly? Comfortably, supported, and never for long in the same way. The perfect posture is a myth that sells chairs. The best chairs are the ones that don't believe in it.
